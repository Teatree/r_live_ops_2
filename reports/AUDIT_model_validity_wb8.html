Fifteen Cracks in the Model

Everything below is measured off workbook (8) and the shipped engine. Ordered by how much it threatens a number you would actually quote.

1–6  changes the answer 7–11  makes a number unreliable 12–15  waiting to break
01
changes the answer

Every leaderboard entrant wins a prize, and half of them podium

The model thinks that if you enter Bomb, Chuck or Red Challenge you finish in the top ten, always — and a coin-flip says you finish top three. Those events are supposed to be competitions. In the model they are near-universal payouts.

The rank ladders are priced over a distribution built from three measured percentiles in data_event_inst, and that distribution is then confined to the ladder’s own depth. So a player who would really finish 80th on a 10-row ladder is modelled as finishing somewhere in 1–10. There is no losing tail at all.

The upstream question is whether position_p50 = 2 is even the right measurement. A median finishing position of second implies boards of about four people. If the SQL is really measuring “rank among players who scored at all”, or a board is segmented far more finely than the model assumes, then every leaderboard payout in the redesign is inflated — and leaderboards are eight of the twenty-nine sources.

What would settle itPull the true board size and the full rank distribution for one event, not three percentiles. If boards are genuinely small, the model is right and these events are simply generous by design — but that should be a stated decision, not a side effect of clamping.

changes the answer

Over half the envelope economy assumes everybody takes part

Night Sky Prize and Rainbow Maker together hand out 57% of all envelopes for a 40-99 payer. Neither has any participation measurement, so the model assumes 100% of players join. Every source that is measured sits between 27% and 92%.

The pack lane prices envelopes as E_v2 × participation × Σ reach. When data_event_inst has no row for a source, packParticipation_ falls through to 1.0. That is the honest default when nothing is known, but it makes the two largest envelope faucets the two least evidenced ones.

03
changes the answer

Kite Festival is priced 8 to 56 times above what players actually do

Measured Kite opt-in in workbook (8) is 0.6% to 4.5%. The model prices it at 35%. That was a deliberate design assumption, and it is still the single largest unvalidated number in the simulation.

PACK_PARTICIPATION = { 'Kite Festival': 0.35 }. It was set to 0.75 and lowered the same day; nothing has validated either figure. It was scoped to the pack lane only, because participation cancels out of the R ratio — but that containment no longer holds. Since the collection rows became a card-sim read-out, more Kite envelopes mean more cards, more completed sets, and more HC. The assumption has quietly escaped into the coin economy.

Cheapest honest movePut a Participation label on Ki_v2 with the number you actually believe. The reader prefers the sheet over the constant, so it needs no code change — and it puts the assumption where a designer can see it.
04
changes the answer

The headline value number cannot see the thing the redesign is about

The block at the very top of Sim per Segment — the one you read first — converts every reward into coins. It has no price for envelopes, no price for ToF tickets, and it prices SPT x2 at zero. So the collection redesign contributes exactly nothing to the number that summarises it.

item_vals carries thirteen resources. The engine produces twenty. Unpriced: SPTx2, all six *-star Pack tiers, and ToF_Ticket. At 40-99 PAYER, 59 of 263 non-zero (source, resource) cells in the grid carry no coin value — 22% of the model’s own output is invisible to any value-weighted view, including the OVERALL VALUE GAINS block and ToF’s “coins-eq” columns.

SPT x2 at 0.0 is separately wrong on its face: a doubled season-pass token is worth twice a token, so about 1.10, not nothing. Any event paying SPT x2 currently reads as paying nothing of value.

changes the answer

The whale segment is modelled as opening the app less often than the one below it

100+ players complete 122 levels on an active day — more than twice what 40-99 players do. Yet the data says they are in the game on fewer days. Every reward that depends on showing up therefore pays your best segment less than your second-best.

This is upstream data, not a bug in the engine — but it propagates everywhere, because reach is built from those two rates. It is why 100+ takes fewer ToF runs than 40-99 (11.7 against 16.3), why the 100+ HC column often trails 40-99, and it makes any statement of the form “the redesign rewards engagement” hard to defend. Either 100+ really is a burst-play segment that logs in rarely and plays enormously, which is worth knowing in its own right, or weekday_active_rate is being computed over a denominator that penalises them.

changes the answer

In Tower of Fortune, ambition is punished

The deeper a segment’s cash-out target, the less it earns and the more it spends. A 0-9 player banks a third of their runs for six coins. A 100+ player banks one run in 700 and pays 28 coins a run to do it. MAX pays 25,924 coins per run to bank one run in 440,000.

The mechanic is faithful — an un-continued Pig loses the whole pot, which the design doc confirms. The policy is what inverts the ordering: the model banks only at exactly the authored cash-out stage and never earlier, so a deeper target is strictly harder to reach and pays strictly less. The sheet’s own Cash-Out Variant input says players may cash out after every successful stage; nothing reads it. Keeping the hard target was a deliberate call on 3 Sep, and the cost of that call is this table.

Nobody finishes the album, so every album reward is dead weight

Your best segment gets 79% of the way through the album and receives nothing. Across 500 simulated players in workbook (8), zero albums were completed. The 1,000-coin album reward is in the config and reaches no one.

Doubling the pool between the two exports is what did it: with 817 copies of 72 cards, each specific card is half as likely per draw, and completion is a threshold. A 6% drop in packs moved the model from “almost always finishes” to “never finishes”. Gold is the binding tier — 41 copies across 10 cards, about 0.5% of the pool per draw.

Worth checking before shippingA reward nobody can reach is worse than no reward: it is a promise the album screen keeps showing. Either the pool comes back down, the envelope flow goes up, or the album reward moves to a reachable milestone.
08
unreliable number

The album reward row is an estimate too coarse to steer by

Album completion is a rare 1,000-coin jackpot. The engine estimates it from 50 simulated seasons, which gives a margin of roughly ±40%. Any config change smaller than that will not show up in the row at all.

On workbook (7) the row shipped 40 HC for 40-99 PAYER where 400 seasons say about 100 — 50 seasons caught two completions instead of five. On (8) it reads 0 everywhere. The number is deterministic (fixed seed, so it does not wobble between recalcs), but deterministic is not the same as accurate. 200 seasons would fix the precision and breach the 30-second custom-function ceiling; COL_SIM_SEASONS is the knob, and reading the menu-run cloud sim instead is the way out.

09
unreliable number

Three sibling events, one config sheet, wildly different opt-in

Bomb, Chuck and Red Challenge are the same event with a different bird. They share one config sheet. Their measured participation is 27%, 92% and 49%. At least one of those is wrong, and Chuck is paying 3.4× Bomb for identical rewards.

All three read Race_v2 and differ only by their data_event_inst key (Bomb / Chuck / Red). A 92% opt-in for an event in the same family as a 27% one points at either a keying problem in the export or a genuine and very large scheduling difference. Combined with finding 01 — 58% of Chuck entrants modelled as top-three — Chuck Challenge is currently the model’s most generous leaderboard on the strength of one unexplained number.

unreliable number

The two simulators disagree about how many ToF runs a player takes

The card simulator spends whole tickets on days the player logged in. The gains model spends a fraction of a ticket every day the event is live. They land on different run counts, and the gap runs both ways.

Neither is wrong in isolation — one is a draw, the other a population mean — but a mean should equal the average of the draws, and here it does not. The gains model lets a player spend part of a ticket on a day when their balance is below one, which the discrete ledger cannot; that biases it high for low-ticket segments and the attendance constraint biases it low for 100+. It is gated as a 35% band, which is an admission rather than a fix.

11
unreliable number

The weekend/weekday Night Sky split does nothing

There is machinery to pay a different Night Sky ladder on weekends than on weekdays. It needs a sheet called NS_v2_weekday. That sheet is not in the workbook, so the whole feature is inert — and Night Sky is the second largest envelope source in the model.

Absent the sheet, E_v2 falls back to NS_v2 for every day. The fallback is deliberate and safe, but it means any reasoning that assumes a weekend-weighted Night Sky payout is describing a model that is not running. Night Sky Prize pays 4.8–9.1 envelopes per season per segment, second only to Rainbow Maker.

12
waiting to break

The Rainbow Maker config split is pinned to instance order

Rainbow Maker’s five calendar slots read two different reward sheets: the first three use RM_1st, the last two RM_2nd. That 3+2 split is hardcoded. Add or remove one Rainbow Maker instance and three of them silently start reading the wrong rewards.

RM_INSTANCE_SHEETS = ["RM_1st","RM_1st","RM_1st","RM_2nd","RM_2nd"], indexed by start-sorted position. Workbook (8) has exactly five instances (days 1-2, 6-9, 13-16, 20-23, 27-30) so it is correct today. There is no check that the count still matches, and Rainbow Maker is the largest envelope source in the model — up to 10.4 packs a season.

13
waiting to break

The headline block is thirteen hand-written cell references

The OVERALL VALUE GAINS block is not written by the engine. It is a spreadsheet formula that names thirteen specific row numbers and thirteen specific item_vals columns. It is correct today. It is also the one part of the sheet no test can reach.

=C26*item_vals!$B$3 + C45*item_vals!$J$3 + C64*item_vals!$I$3 + … — rows 26, 45, 64, 83, 102, 121, 140, 159, 178, 197, 216, 235, 254 against item_vals columns B, J, I, H, E, F, G, N, M, L, K, C, D. I verified every pairing resolves to the right resource. But resources have been appended to the engine three times this year, and each time every block below the insertion point moves. When that happens, this formula keeps returning a plausible number computed from the wrong rows — the exact failure that produced the Sim per Segment grouping bug and the pack-log column bug.

The structural fixLet the engine write this block too, deriving positions from RESOURCES, and gate it. It is the last positional coupling left on the sheet.
14
waiting to break

The “PAID” column contains no purchases

The PAID group on Sim per Segment reads 300 HC for both 40-99 and 100+ payers. All of it is the paid season pass. IAPs pays zero HC in every segment — the model has no in-app-purchase currency at all.

Two consequences. A column labelled PAID that excludes purchases will be misread by anyone who did not write it. And the paid pass being identical for 40-99 and 100+ (300 HC both) means the model has no notion of a whale buying more passes or higher tiers — the two segments are indistinguishable on the one axis where they should differ most. Worth remembering that the paid track is also assumed available to every payer, which is a flagged assumption rather than a measurement.

15
waiting to break

The grid now runs a card simulation inside every cell

Since the collection rows started running the real card simulator, each EcoGainsSim cell simulates 50 player-seasons. That is an estimated 2 to 13 seconds per cell against a hard 30-second limit on spreadsheet functions.

Measured 0.21–0.64s per cell in node; Google Apps Script runs roughly 5–20× slower. It is comfortably inside the ceiling today, and there is no margin for a bigger album, more sources, or a raised COL_SIM_SEASONS. When a custom function does breach the limit it does not fall back — the cell errors, and neighbouring cells that succeeded still show numbers, so the sheet looks partly fine.

What I did not count as a finding. The eight rarity-less rows in AlbumConfig are sub-table headers and are correctly ignored (72 cards, eight sets of nine). cal_parsed agrees with a live parse of cal_new, so the precompute is not stale. The A. 0 segment is inert by design — zero players, zero activity rates. And Sim per Segment reproduces the engine to the decimal on every group column I checked, including ToF inside META.
